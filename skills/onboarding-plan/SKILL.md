# Onboarding plan

A dated plan for a new person's first ninety days, with real checkpoints and an honest
review built in.

Small businesses onboard by sitting someone next to the owner and hoping. Ninety days
later nobody can say whether it worked, and by then it is expensive.

## Read the brief

Look for `business-brief.md` in the working directory or the project's files.

- **Found:** read it for what the business does, its constraints and anything a new person
  could get badly wrong in week one.
- **Not found:** carry on. Ask what the business does and what the role is. Mention
  `business-brief` once at the end.

Also look for **`scorecard-<role>.md`** and any **`sop-<process>.md`** files. If a scorecard
exists, the 90-day checkpoints should build toward its outcomes. If SOPs exist, schedule
them — an onboarding plan that ignores documentation the business already has is wasteful.

## What it needs

- **The role, and the start date.** Everything is dated from it.
- **Who is responsible for them.** Not "the team". A named person. Onboarding with no owner
  does not happen.
- **How much time that person can actually give**, honestly. A plan assuming two hours a
  day from an owner who has twenty minutes is a plan that gets abandoned in week one.

## Process

### 1. Work out what they need in the first three days

Access, tools, a place to sit, someone to ask. List it as a pre-start checklist with the
date each item must be done by. Most onboarding failures are a login that did not exist on
Monday morning.

### 2. Decide what "useful" looks like in week one

One real thing they can complete and hand over in the first week. Not shadowing, not
reading. People who produce something in week one stay; people who watch for a fortnight
start looking for the exit.

Pick something genuinely small and genuinely real.

### 3. Phase the ninety days

Propose a shape and confirm it. A workable default:

- **Days 1–14** — the basics, first real task, meet whoever they must
- **Days 15–45** — owning a defined piece of work, with supervision
- **Days 46–90** — running it, with the owner stepping back

For each phase: what they should be able to do unaided by the end, what they are given, and
who they check in with.

### 4. Put the checkpoints in as dated meetings

End of week one, end of week two, day thirty, day sixty, day ninety. Short, with a stated
question each. "How is it going" is not a checkpoint; "which parts of this are still
unclear" is.

Put them in as real dates, calculated from the start date.

### 5. Write the "not working out" review

The part that gets skipped and the part that matters most. Around day thirty, an explicit,
scheduled, two-way review of whether this is working — with the signals written down in
advance, before anyone is emotionally invested.

Say plainly that it is a normal part of the plan and that both sides can use it. A business
that has never written this down is a business that keeps people six months too long.

**Note where employment law applies.** Probation terms, notice and dismissal are regulated
and vary by jurisdiction. Write the plan, and say plainly that the formal probation and
notice terms are for their contract and, if in doubt, an employment adviser. Never state a
legal notice period or probation right as fact.

### 6. Say what they should know by day ninety

A short list. It doubles as the day-ninety agenda.

## Output

Write to **`onboarding-<role>.md`**.

```markdown
# Onboarding — <role>, starting <date>

**Responsible for them:** <named person>
**Time they can give:** <honest amount>

## Before day one
- [ ] <item> — by <date> — <who>

## Week one
**First real task:** <the thing they complete and hand over>

| Day | What happens | Who |
|---|---|---|

## Days 15–45
**Able to do unaided by the end:** <what>
- <what they are given, and who supervises>

## Days 46–90
**Able to do unaided by the end:** <what>

## Checkpoints
| Date | Question |
|---|---|
| <date> | <the specific question> |

## The day-30 review
**<date>** — an honest two-way conversation about whether this is working.

**Signs it is going well:** <written now, before anyone is invested>
**Signs it is not:** <same>

<Note that formal probation and notice terms belong in the contract, and to check with an
employment adviser if unsure.>

## By day 90 they should know
- <item>
```

## Quality bar

**Good:** the new person could read it and know what is expected of them, and the owner
could follow it in the time they actually have.

**Failed:** a reading list and a series of introductions. If the plan contains nothing the
person produces in the first fortnight, it will be abandoned.

Never invent a legal notice period, a probation right, a statutory entitlement or a
required training certification. Say what is regulated and point them at their contract or
an adviser. Never schedule more of the owner's time than they said they had. Close by
listing gaps and saying how many there are.
