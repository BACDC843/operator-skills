# Proposal writer

A proposal for one client and one job, with the scope, the exclusions and the price all
stated plainly.

Proposals fail on exclusions. The work that was never quoted is the work that becomes an
argument in week six.

## Read the brief

Look for `business-brief.md` in the working directory or the project's files.

- **Found:** read it. The offer, price, exclusions, constraints, proof and voice all come
  from there. The **Not included** lines in the brief are the single most useful thing here.
- **Not found:** carry on. Ask for the offer, the price and what the client asked for.
  Mention `business-brief` once at the end.

Markers bind. Never resolve a `[withheld]` or `[gap]` price by estimating — a made-up number
in a proposal is a number the client will hold them to.

## What it needs

- **Who it is for**, by name, and what they actually asked for. Paste of their email or
  enquiry is ideal.
- **The price**, or an explicit decision to quote a range or a day rate.
- **What is in scope**, and anything discussed that is not.

If any of the three is missing, ask. A proposal is the highest-consequence document in the
pack — it is a number a stranger may hold the business to.

## Process

### 1. Restate what they asked for, in their words

Open by showing you understood the problem. Quote their own phrasing where you have it.
A client reading their own words in paragraph one stops checking whether you listened.

### 2. Write the scope as deliverables, not activities

"Four blog posts of 800–1,000 words, delivered fortnightly" is scope. "Content marketing"
is a category. Every line should be something the client could point at and say whether it
arrived.

### 3. Write the exclusions, and do not soften them

The section that earns the skill. Include:

- What was discussed but is not in this price
- What the client must supply, and by when — the commonest cause of an overrun is a client
  who did not send something
- What happens if the scope changes, in one plain sentence
- Anything the brief's Constraints section says they do not do

Pull exclusions from the brief's **Not included** lines. If the brief has none, say so and
ask — a proposal with no exclusions is not finished.

### 4. State the price, and what it is not

The number, what it covers, when it is payable, and what would change it. If the price is a
range, say what puts a job at each end. Never present a range where the top is decorative.

### 5. Say what happens next

One or two steps with a date, and a decision-by date if the price has a shelf life. Only
state a shelf life if the user confirms it is real.

### 6. Keep proof honest and short

Two or three relevant things from the brief's Proof section. Named clients only if the
brief says they are cleared. If Proof is `[none supplied]`, write no proof section rather
than filling one — an invented credential in a proposal is fraud, not marketing.

## Output

Write to **`proposal-<client>.md`**, following
[references/proposal-template.md](references/proposal-template.md).

Keep it to about two pages. A long proposal reads as a business that is not sure what it
charges.

**This skill drafts. It does not send.** When the proposal is finished, say where the file
is and that it is ready for them to review and send. Never offer to email it — a proposal
is a number a client will hold them to, and the last read belongs to them.

## Quality bar

**Good:** a client could accept it and both sides would agree, six weeks later, on what was
bought. The price appears once, clearly, with no hedging around it.

**Failed:** an exclusions section that says "anything not listed above". That is not an
exclusion, it is a phrase, and it will not survive the conversation it exists to prevent.

Two rules that override everything else:

- **Never invent a price, a timeline, a team size, a credential or a client name.** Mark it
  `[gap]` and tell the user which gaps must be closed before sending.
- **Never quote a delivery date you were not given.** Ask, or leave it marked.

Close by listing every gap that must be filled before this goes to the client, and say how
many there are.
