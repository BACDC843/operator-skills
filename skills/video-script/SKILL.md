# Video script

A script someone can actually read out, with a hook that earns the next five seconds and a
read time that is true.

Most "video scripts" are essays with line breaks. Nobody can perform one, and the read time
is always double what was claimed.

## Read the brief

Look for `business-brief.md` in the working directory or the project's files.

- **Found:** read it. Voice matters more here than anywhere else in the pack — this is the
  owner's own mouth, and a script they would not say is a script they will not film.
- **Not found:** carry on. Ask what the business does and what the video is about. Mention
  `business-brief` once at the end.

## What it needs

- **Short or long.** A 30-second vertical and an 8-minute YouTube video are different jobs.
  Ask, or propose based on the subject and confirm.
- **Who is on camera**, if anyone. A piece to camera, a voiceover over footage and a
  screen recording are scripted differently.
- **The one thing the viewer should leave with.** If there is more than one, it is more
  than one video.

## Process

### 1. Write the hook last, but put it first

The first line has one job: make the next line worth staying for. Write three hook options
and say which you would use and why. Do not write a hook that the video does not pay off —
a promise the script fails to keep is worse than a flat opening.

Things that work: the specific claim, the correction of a common belief, the number, the
thing that went wrong. Things that do not: "In this video I'm going to…", a greeting, the
business name.

### 2. Structure to the length

- **Short form (under 60s)** — hook, one point, one piece of proof, one ask. That is all
  that fits. Cutting to one point is the work.
- **Long form** — hook, why it matters to this viewer, the substance in three or four
  beats, then the ask. Signpost the beats so a viewer who joins late can follow.

### 3. Write it to be spoken

Short sentences. Contractions. The words the owner actually uses, taken from the brief's
voice excerpts. Read it back in your head — anything you stumble over, they will stumble
over on camera.

Mark natural pauses and where a cut or a change of shot goes. Do not write stage directions
they cannot execute alone with a phone.

### 4. Give an honest read time

Count it at roughly **140 words a minute** for a natural speaking pace, and say the number.
If the script runs over the target, cut it rather than reporting the real time as if the
target were met. A 45-second script that claims to be 30 is a wasted shoot.

### 5. Write what goes around it

The title, the description, and the on-screen text. On-screen text is not the script
repeated — it is the two or three phrases that carry the meaning with the sound off, which
is how most of it will be watched.

## Output

Write to **`script-<subject>.md`**.

```markdown
# <Subject> — <format, length>

**One thing the viewer leaves with:** <sentence>
**Read time:** <honest count> at a natural pace (<n> words)

## Hook options
1. <option> ← recommended, because <reason>
2. <option>
3. <option>

## Script

**[hook]**
<line>

**[cut / new shot]**
<line>

**[ask]**
<line>

## On-screen text
- <phrase>

## Title
<title>

## Description
<description, with the ask repeated>
```

## Quality bar

**Good:** the owner could read it aloud once, unrehearsed, and sound like themselves. The
read time is accurate. The hook is a promise the script keeps.

**Failed:** written prose broken into lines. If a sentence has a subordinate clause nobody
would say out loud, rewrite it.

Never invent a statistic or a result to strengthen a hook — a hook is the most-seen line in
the pack and the worst place for an unverifiable claim. Close by saying what you could not
do, including any footage the script assumes exists.
